Abstract

Because many proteins in plasma/serum are altered in Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD), a panel of proteins can be a blood biomarker for ASD. Therefore, in this module, we investigated how preprocessing, outlier removal, and protein selection methods affect a classification model that distinguishes between individuals with Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) and those who are typically developing (TD). We found that log transformation reduced the right skewness of the protein distributions and that TD individuals typically had more outliers on average in comparison to ASD individuals. Furthermore, changes in protein selection can change how a model performs, suggesting possible overfitting in the original analysis. Finally, we improved our classifier and increased accuracy from 0.746 to 0.783 and AUC from 0.828 to 0.877. Overall, our findings highlight the importance of preprocessing and protein selection when improving ASD classification models.

Dataset

The dataset from Hewitson et al. (2021) contained 1,125 proteins used as a panel for Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) prediction in boys. The sample, which was collected via a blood test, included 76 boys with ASD and 78 typically developing (TD) boys, between 18 months and 8 years of age. While 1,317 were originally collected per subject, 192 proteins failed quality control so 1,125 were used in the publication. However, these 192 failed variables are still included in our analysis by default.

The data was preprocessed with log transformation, standardization, outlier trimming, and quality control. The log transformation reduced the right skewness of the protein distributions. The standardization converted measurements to z-scores to make them comparable to each other. Outlier trimming was used to reduce the influence of extreme values by limiting standardized protein measurements to the range of −3 to +3.

Summary of published analysis

The dataset from Hewitson et al. (2021) was preprocessed with a log transformation, a standardization, and an outlier trimming. Then three methods identified proteins, namely random forests, t-tests, and correlation analysis with ASD severity scores. Afterward, 10 proteins from each of the prior methods were used to identify a smaller group of potential proteins to use for analysis. The five proteins that were picked were MAPK14, IgD, DERM, EPHB2, and suPAR. After adding four other proteins (ROR1, GI24, eIF-4H, and ARSB) to improve performance they used logistic regression as a form of classification with a repeated 80/20 training/testing split. As a result, they got an AUC of .860, sensitivity of 83.5%, and specificity of 82.2%

However, it should be noted that there was an error in the analysis which caused the paper to be retracted as the results were unable to be replicated. Furthermore, the TD subjects were assigned random ASD severity scores. This indicates that the original findings were not reliable.

Findings

Impact of preprocessing and outliers

To investigate what preprocessing did we first examined what the raw distribution of the protein levels looked like in comparison to the log-transformed numbers. After analysis it was determined that a log transformation was applied because a lot of the data was right skewed. This means that while most subjects had low proteins, some did have high. In order to reduce the influence of the extremes they needed to be compressed with log transformation. However, it should be noted that even after a log transformation, not every protein is normally distributed, and some are left as right skewed.

After, we examined the what would happen if you removed the outlier trimming from the preprocessing code. First we counted how many outlying protein values the subjects had individually while considering any value greater than 3 or less than 3 to be an outlier. We did this to test is outliers were more common in the ASD or the TD group. It was determined that there were a handful of subjects (namely 154, 108, 9, 121, 52, and 77 had the most) with significantly higher outlines ranging from 114 to 157 outliers. These subjects came from both groups indicating that the outliers were spread among all participants. After comparing ASD (1007 outliers) which averaged 13.2 outliers per subject and TD (1372 outliers) which had 17.6 outliers per subject, it could be seen that TD subjects had more on average. Furthermore, 75 out of 76 ASD subjects and 77 out of 78 TD subjects, had at least one outlier, indicating the difference did not come from how many subjects in each group had an outlier. However, it should be noted that 7 out of 10 of the subjects with the most outliers came out of TD suggesting that a high number of outliers is more common in the TD group. Overall, most subjects had at least one outlier even if certain subjects had significantly more than others. While the TD group had more outliers on average it is entirely possible that those with high outliers made the average rise.

For our modifications in protein selection, we first took a 71/29 training and testing split and conducted the selection procedure only on the training partition. Comparing the original and training partition metric set, every estimated value was significantly lower than our initial findings. The smallest metric difference, specificity, decreased by approximately 9.7%, while the largest metric decrease, sensitivity, fell approximately 39%. Overall, the modified model performs worse than the original analysis. Since the initial metrics were calculated using the entire dataset for protein/variable selection, this stark difference may be explained in part from overfitting to the data. Calculating with the entire dataset again, we explored increasing the amount of predictive proteins. The original method involved a hard intersection of the top 10 random forest and 10 t-test proteins, giving our baseline predictors DERM, RELT, and IgD. We decided on increasing the candidate proteins from each selection method to 25 and acquired 12 additional proteins. This offered a net improvement in specificity, accuracy, and ROC AUC, with specificity increasing the most by 20% but sensitivity falling 12.5%. Lastly, we applied a fuzzy intersection. The proteins were ranked based on their respective Gini importance or adj. p values. Then, we calculated the protein’s random forest and t-test membership scores through min-max normalization, and assigned the minimum score among the two as the protein’s final score. This intersection method gave 10 proteins including the baseline, performing worse relative to the 25 protein subset method. Specificity and ROC AUC increased by the same amount, but there was no effect on accuracy, and a greater decrease in sensitivity by 18.8%. We also performed a second fuzzy intersection, where we subtracted 1 by each protein’s t-test p-value and combined it with their random forest importance values. We took the difference between 1 and p-value so that larger numbers would mean better attributes for selection, and by taking the average between it and importance, we attempted to find the overall most influential proteins in both methods. Then, we selected the top 10 proteins with the highest average values and proceeded with the analysis as usual. In comparison to our initial fuzzy intersection, sensitivity further decreased at 25%, and accuracy slightly decreased unlike the previous one which had no difference. Our specificity and ROC AUC metrics share similar increases with the first fuzzy intersection.

Improved classifier

Our methodology was to use forward selection from the 30 proteins with the smallest t-test p-values, adding the protein that improved 5-fold cross-validated AUC, stopping when the gain fell below 0.005, similar to what the published paper conducted. We found that the new panel that best improved AUC was as follows: DERM, MAPK2, IgD, P-Cadherin and PTN. The original panel had the following proteins: DERM, RELT, and IgD. Like in the in-class analysis, the panel was selected using the full dataset, so the test data were also seen during both panels’ accuracy estimates are likely optimistic to some degree.

With only 31 subjects, the single-split accuracy gain amounts to about one additional correct classification, so we also averaged over 30 random splits. Both panels score lower when averaged over 30 splits than on the in-class split. However, the new panel outperforms the in-class panel in both settings.

With this new panel, from the single split metrics we found an improvement in accuracy from 0.806 to 0.839 (+0.033), and an increase in AUC from 0.892 to 0.975 (+0.083). As for the 30-split average, accuracy rose from 0.746 to 0.783 (+0.037), and AUC grew from 0.828 to 0.877 (+0.049). This tells us that the new panel improves the accuracy and AUC by a non-negligible margin. The improvement comes from specificity (+0.200, +0.101) at the cost of some sensitivity (-0.125, -0.034).

Our initial implementation of the classification model of the same procedure utilized Python and yielded a similar panel (DERM, IgD, MAPK2, FSTL1, P-Cadherin, and EPHB2, four proteins in common) with comparable performance (0.790 for accuracy and 0.874 for AUC on repeated split). The contrasts likely stem from how R and Python form cross-validation folds differently.
